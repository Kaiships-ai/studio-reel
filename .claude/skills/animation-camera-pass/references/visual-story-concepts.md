# Visual Story Concepts

Background theory behind the camera pass. Summaries are written in our own words. Read the original books for depth; do not paste book text into project files.

---

## 1. Bruce Block: The Visual Story

### 1.1 The seven visual components
Every shot is built from the same seven components. The camera controls most of them directly.

| Component | What it means for camera | Camera levers |
|---|---|---|
| Space | How deep or flat the frame feels | Lens, camera height, depth layers, convergence of lines |
| Line | Real and implied lines (horizon, edges, eyelines, motion paths) | Angle, dutch, composition |
| Shape | Basic shapes the frame reduces to (circle, square, triangle) | Framing, angle, silhouette |
| Tone | Brightness range | Mostly lighting; camera chooses what fills the frame |
| Color | Hue, saturation, warm vs cool | Mostly lighting / design; camera chooses what dominates |
| Movement | Movement of objects, of the camera, and of the viewer's eye | Camera moves, staging, cut points |
| Rhythm | Repetition and alternation in space and time | Shot length, cut pattern, repeated framing |

### 1.2 Contrast and affinity
- **Affinity** = similar visual components across shots (same size, same lens, same angle). Lowers visual intensity. Feels calm, unified, sometimes flat.
- **Contrast** = differing components (wide to extreme close-up, eye level to low angle, static to fast move). Raises visual intensity. Feels energetic, tense.
- The rule: **visual intensity should track story intensity.** Use affinity where the story is calm, build contrast as the story rises, peak at the climax, release after.
- Contrast can act inside a shot (deep foreground vs flat background), between consecutive shots, or across a whole sequence.

### 1.3 Story intensity vs visual intensity graph
- Plot story intensity per shot or beat, then plot visual intensity on the same axis.
- When the curves match, the sequence feels right even if the audience cannot say why.
- Common mismatches: visual peak before story climax; visual flat while story rises; visual high where the story needs rest.
- This graph is the core tool of the skill (SKILL.md Step 2).

### 1.4 Types of space
| Space | Look | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Deep | Strong perspective, wide lens, many depth layers, diagonal lines | Energy, scale, danger |
| Flat | Long lens, frontal planes, little depth | Calm, formal, or claustrophobic |
| Limited | Depth from size and overlap only, frontal angle | Controlled, stylized |
| Ambiguous | Viewer cannot read scale or position | Disorientation, dream, horror |

Pick one space type per sequence or story section and change it on purpose.

## 2. Steven Katz: Film Directing Shot by Shot
- **Floor plan first.** Plan character positions, movement and every camera setup from overhead before drawing frames.
- **Coverage** = the set of setups that lets the editor build the scene (master, singles, overs, inserts). In animation you do not shoot coverage; you choose the single best shot. Plan as if covering, then commit.
- **Staging in depth**: move characters toward and away from camera to change shot size inside one setup instead of cutting.
- **Camera follows the scene's dramatic structure**: setups tighten as the scene's tension rises.

## 3. Joseph Mascelli: The Five C's of Cinematography
| C | Question to ask per shot |
|---|---|
| Camera angles | Does the angle express the character's power and the viewer's position? |
| Continuity | Do direction, eyelines and positions survive the cut? |
| Cutting | Is there a reason to cut here (new information, reaction, action)? |
| Close-ups | Is the close-up saved for when it matters? |
| Composition | Is there one clear focal point and a readable frame? |

## 4. Walter Murch: Rule of Six (In the Blink of an Eye)
Priority order when deciding a cut. If a cut must sacrifice something, sacrifice from the bottom up.

1. Emotion (most important)
2. Story
3. Rhythm
4. Eye trace (where the eye is at the cut and where it lands next)
5. Two-dimensional plane of the screen (screen direction, 180-degree line)
6. Three-dimensional space of action (spatial continuity)

Use it to break ties: a cut that crosses the line but lands the emotion can be right; a technically clean cut that loses the emotion is wrong.

## 5. Animation-specific notes
- The animation camera has no physical limits, so audiences read impossible moves as "CG". Keep camera behavior physical (mass, ease in/out, real rigs) unless the style is deliberately non-physical.
- Animators "cheat to camera": once the camera is locked, character positions can be adjusted for the frame. This is why the camera must lock before animation.
- Staging (Disney principle 3) lives mostly in camera and layout. A pose that reads in silhouette is the test.
- In a short-form or vertical (9:16) delivery, re-check every composition: headroom, lead room and the thirds change completely.
